01 · Situations

A hard time can weigh more when the people you'd lean on are far away.

  1. 01

    Someone in your family is seriously ill

    It may be a physical illness or a mental one, in a parent, a partner, a sibling or a child. If you're far away, you get news second-hand and in fragments, and every decision about whether to fly back carries guilt either way. If they're here with you, you may be doing most of the caring alone, with doctors who speak a language you're still learning.

  2. 02

    You've lost someone, and the people around you didn't know them

    Maybe you couldn't get to the funeral, or you went and came back to a life where nobody had met the person you're grieving. Grief doesn't follow the timetable of a working week, and it can be lonelier when the people who'd share it are in another country.

  3. 03

    A relationship or a job has ended, and so has the reason you came

    If you moved for a partner or a job, losing either one can raise a bigger question: whether to stay at all. You're dealing with the loss and with a decision about your whole life at the same time.

  4. 04

    Trying for a baby is taking longer than you hoped

    Tests and treatment bring months of waiting, often with appointments and results in Italian. Each month can bring hope and then disappointment, and it isn't always easy to talk about with the people around you.

  5. 05

    A baby is on the way, or has just arrived, and your own family is far away

    Sleepless nights come without anyone to drop round with food or take a turn. The mix of happiness and worry can be hard to share when the people you'd usually share it with are a flight away.

  6. 06

    The move itself is heavier than you expected

    It was your choice, and by most measures it's going well. Still, you feel off balance, you miss people more than you thought you would, and small things that used to be easy now take effort.

None of these stays neatly in one part of your life. A bereavement shows up at your desk, and losing a job changes how things are at home, so the sessions can cover both.

02 · How it works

Psychological support with Monica focuses on what's happening now.

Proposed method: to confirm with Monica.

Psychological support focuses on getting through a specific hard time. It looks at the situation you're in now and how you're coping with it, rather than at difficulties that go back a long way.

Usually shorter than psychotherapy; how long, with Monica: to confirm.

  1. 01

    The first sessions: what's happening and what you need

    Monica asks about what's going on and how it's affecting you day to day, including who you can count on, here and at home. Together you agree what you'd like the sessions to help with.

    Assessment tools or questionnaires, if any: to confirm with Monica.

  2. 02

    Then sessions shaped around your situation

    Some sessions are space to say what you can't say elsewhere. Others are more practical: how to manage sleep and worry; how to make a decision you keep putting off, such as whether to travel home; how to talk to the people around you, an employer included, about what you're going through.

    Specific techniques: to confirm with Monica.

  3. 03

    A check on how it's going

    Along the way, you and Monica look at whether the sessions are helping and what you still need. If something longer would help more, she will tell you (see below).

    When support ends, and whether you can stop at any time: to confirm with Monica.

03 · What it covers

What psychological support with Monica can cover.

  • Caring for a family member with a serious physical or mental illness, nearby or from a distance.
  • Bereavement, including grieving far from the people who knew the person.
  • Separation or divorce.
  • Losing a job, and what it changes beyond work.
  • Infertility, through tests, treatment and waiting.
  • Becoming a parent, from pregnancy to the first months with a baby.
  • Moving and living away from home, when settling in is harder than expected.

04 · When something different would help

When something different would help.

  • When support isn't enough. If what comes up turns out to run deeper, or was there long before this hard time, Monica may suggest psychotherapy, which she also offers. You decide whether to go on. Psychotherapy
  • When the question is about work. If what you need is help with your career or your job, Monica can suggest Elsa. With your consent, they can talk to each other.
  • To confirm with the practice. How information is shared, and its limits: to define with the practice; check with your own adviser.
  • Medical and practical matters. Medical questions about your own health or your relative's, legal questions after a separation and paperwork after a death need the relevant doctor, lawyer or office.
  • Medication and emergencies. Blooming doesn't offer psychiatry. If you or someone else is in danger, call 112 or 118, or go to the nearest emergency department (Pronto Soccorso).
